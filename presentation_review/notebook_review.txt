# PART 1 — CONCEPTS

## 1. Objective

Find the **global minimum of the 2-D Ackley function** using a **Genetic Algorithm**.

In plain words: there is a bumpy mathematical landscape with one deepest point, and we must find it without
ever being told where it is — only by asking the landscape "how deep are you here?" at points we choose.

| The GA **may** use | The GA **may not** use |
|---|---|
| the search range $-5 \le x_1,x_2 \le 5$ | the location of the answer, $(0,0)$ |
| the value of $f$ at any point it picks | any derivative or algebraic shortcut |

**What we must report at the end:** best $x_1$, best $x_2$, best $f(x_1,x_2)$, the generation where that best
was found, and the error against the true optimum.

## 2. The Ackley Function

### 2.1 General form for $n$ dimensions

$$
f(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=
-a\exp\!\left(-b\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i^{2}}\right)
-\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\cos(c\,x_i)\right)
+a+e,
\qquad a=20,\; b=0.2,\; c=2\pi .
$$

### 2.2 Deriving the 2-D version

With $n=2$ the two summations open up:

$$\frac{1}{n}\sum x_i^{2}\;\longrightarrow\;\frac{x_1^{2}+x_2^{2}}{2},
\qquad
\frac{1}{n}\sum \cos(c\,x_i)\;\longrightarrow\;\frac{\cos(2\pi x_1)+\cos(2\pi x_2)}{2}.$$

Substituting back gives the function this notebook minimises:

$$
\boxed{\;
f(x_1,x_2)=
-20\exp\!\left(-0.2\sqrt{\tfrac{x_1^{2}+x_2^{2}}{2}}\right)
-\exp\!\left(\tfrac{\cos(2\pi x_1)+\cos(2\pi x_2)}{2}\right)
+20+e \;}
$$

### 2.3 The known answer

$$x^{*}=(0,0),\qquad f(x^{*})=0 .$$

Check it by hand: at the origin the square-root term is $0$, so the first part is $-20e^{0}=-20$; both cosines
are $1$, so the second part is $-e$. Adding $+20+e$ leaves exactly $0$.

We use this **only to measure our error at the end**. The algorithm never sees it.

=== CELL 3 markdown ===
---

## 3. Why This Function Is Hard

| Piece | Shape it creates |
|---|---|
| $-20\exp(-0.2\sqrt{\cdot\,})$ | one wide, smooth funnel sloping towards the origin |
| $-\exp\!\left(\tfrac{\cos 2\pi x_1+\cos 2\pi x_2}{2}\right)$ | a grid of small ripples laid over that funnel |

The result is **multimodal**: one global minimum at the centre, surrounded by rings of local minima about one
unit apart, near integer coordinates.

* **Gradient descent** only walks downhill, so it settles in the first ripple it meets and reports a local
  minimum as the answer.
* **Grid search** to 6-decimal accuracy over $[-5,5]^2$ would need roughly $10^{14}$ evaluations.

A GA avoids both traps because it keeps a **whole population** spread over the space and keeps injecting
randomness, so some individuals are always outside the ripple that trapped the others.

=== CELL 4 markdown ===
---

## 4. What a Genetic Algorithm Is

A GA is a **search and optimisation technique based on Darwin's principle of natural selection**. The whole
philosophy fits in one line from the lecture slides: **"Select the best, discard the rest."**

Keep a population of candidate answers, score them, let the good ones have more children, mix and randomly
disturb those children, repeat. Over generations the population drifts towards good regions — the way a
species adapts to its environment.

| Nature | Computer (this assignment) |
|---|---|
| Population | a set of 50 candidate solutions |
| Individual | one candidate solution — a point in the plane |
| Chromosome | its encoding, `[x1, x2]` |
| Gene | one coordinate, `x1` or `x2` |
| Fitness | quality of the solution (here: how small $f$ is) |
| Reproduction | crossover |
| Environmental pressure | selection + elitism |

**Why a GA fits this problem:** it needs no derivative, it searches many ripples at once, and mutation lets a
candidate escape a ripple it has fallen into. The lecture slides use Ackley's function itself as the standard
example of a "hard problem" for evolutionary computation.

## 5. Chromosome Representation — Value Encoding

The assignment requires the **value (real-valued) encoding technique**.

$$\text{Chromosome}=[\,x_1,\;x_2\,]$$

```
          gene 1    gene 2
        +---------+---------+
        |   x1    |   x2    |
        +---------+---------+
```

* Exactly **2 genes**, because the problem has 2 variables.
* Each gene stores the **actual floating-point number**, never a binary string.
* Every gene must always satisfy $-5\le x_i\le 5$.

Examples from the assignment sheet: $[-4.0,\;2.5]$ and $[-1.5,\;-1.0]$.

**Why not binary encoding?** It would force us to fix the bit-width in advance, which puts a hard floor under
the precision we can ever reach — a 20-bit gene over $[-5,5]$ cannot express anything finer than about
$10^{-5}$. With real values, precision is limited only by the mutation step size, which we can shrink as the
search progresses.

=== CELL 6 markdown ===
---

## 6. Objective Value versus Fitness

This is the one place a minimisation problem needs care.

* **Objective value** $f(x_1,x_2)$ — **lower is better**.
* **Fitness** — what roulette-wheel selection turns into probability, so **higher must be better**.

We cannot feed $f$ straight into the wheel; we must flip its direction first. We use the classic **windowing**
transform, where $f_{\max}$ and $f_{\min}$ are the worst and best values *in the current population*:

$$F_i=\big(f_{\max}-f_i\big)+\delta,
\qquad \delta=0.10\,\big(f_{\max}-f_{\min}\big)+\varepsilon,
\qquad p_i=\frac{F_i}{\sum_j F_j}.$$

* the best chromosome gets the widest window, so the largest slice;
* the worst would get exactly $0$ and could never be picked, so the offset $\delta$ leaves it a thin but
  non-zero slice, preserving diversity;
* $\varepsilon$ keeps everything defined if the population has converged to one value.

A second common transform, $F_i = 1/(1+f_i)$, is also implemented so the two can be compared in §27.

## 7. The Genetic Operators

### 7.1 Selection — Roulette Wheel

A wheel is cut into 50 slices, one per chromosome, where **slice size = selection probability $p_i$**. Spin it
50 times to fill the mating pool. We build the rim as a cumulative array:

```
p      = [0.05, 0.12, 0.03, 0.20, ...]     selection probabilities
wheel  = [0.05, 0.17, 0.20, 0.40, ...]     running total  (the rim)
spin   =  0.18                             one random number in [0, 1)
result =  index 3, because 0.17 <= 0.18 < 0.20
```

A good chromosome owns a large slice and is picked often; a weak one still owns a thin slice, so diversity
survives. This distribution is exactly what a **pie chart** draws, which is why the assignment asks for one.

### 7.2 Crossover — One Point, $P_c = 80\%$

With two genes there is exactly **one legal cut: between $x_1$ and $x_2$**.

```
Parent 1 :  [ a | b ]              Child 1 : [ a | d ]
Parent 2 :  [ c | d ]      ==>     Child 2 : [ c | b ]
                ^ the single cut point
```

So crossover here means *take the $x_1$ of one parent and the $x_2$ of the other*. It cannot invent a new
number — it only **re-combines coordinates the population already has**. That is its job: if one parent holds a
good $x_1$ and another holds a good $x_2$, crossover puts them in one child in a single step. With probability
$1-P_c=20\%$ no cut is made and the children are copies of the parents.

### 7.3 Mutation — $P_m = 5\%$ per gene

Mutation is the only operator that can **create a value the population has never held**. We walk the chromosome
gene by gene (*array traversal*) and, for each gene independently, draw a random number; if it is below $0.05$
that gene is changed, then **clipped** back into $[-5,5]$ so no chromosome can leave the search space.

| Mode | Rule | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Gaussian** (default) | $x_i \leftarrow x_i + \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})$ | a small nudge — good for fine tuning |
| **Uniform** | $x_i \leftarrow \mathcal{U}(-5,5)$ | a completely fresh value — a big jump |

Example from the assignment sheet: $[-1.5,-1.0]$ may mutate to $[-0.5,-1.0]$ — gene 1 changed, gene 2 untouched.

### 7.4 Elitism — 1 individual

Crossover and mutation are random, so the best chromosome can easily be destroyed. Elitism copies it into the
next generation untouched. The consequence matters: **the best-so-far curve can never go up.**

## 8. Choosing the Mutation Step Size

The mutation *probability* is fixed at 5% by the assignment. The *step size* $\sigma$ is ours to choose, and it
turns out to be the single most important decision in the whole implementation, so it gets its own section.

### 8.1 Why a fixed timetable is not good enough

The obvious approach is a schedule on the clock: start at $\sigma=1$ and shrink geometrically to $10^{-8}$ by
generation 100. Large steps early to explore, tiny steps late to polish. We built that first, and it worked —
but it has a flaw that only shows up when you run it many times.

**The step size shrinks whether or not the search is making progress.** If a run is still stuck in the wrong
ripple at generation 40, $\sigma$ has already fallen to about $10^{-3}$ — far too small to jump the distance of
roughly 1 unit needed to escape. The run is then locked into a local minimum for the rest of its life. Over 40
independent runs this happened to one of them, which ended at $f \approx 2.58$ instead of $\approx 0$.

### 8.2 The fix: tie the step size to the current solution quality

Instead of asking *"how late is it?"*, ask *"how far away are we still?"*:

$$\boxed{\;\sigma \;=\; \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{4}\,f_{\text{best}},\; \sigma_{\min},\; \sigma_{\max}\right)\;}$$

**Where the $\tfrac14$ comes from.** Near the origin the Ackley function is almost a straight cone. Writing
$s=\sqrt{(x_1^2+x_2^2)/2}$ and expanding both exponentials for small $s$:

$$-20e^{-0.2s}\approx-20+4s,
\qquad -e^{\,1-2\pi^2s^2}\approx-e+2e\pi^2s^2,
\qquad\text{so}\qquad f \approx 4s + 2e\pi^2 s^2 \approx 4s .$$

So $s \approx f/4$: dividing the current best value by 4 gives, near the optimum, **the distance that still has
to be covered**. The mutation step is therefore always about the size of the remaining gap.

### 8.3 What this buys us

| Situation | $f_{\text{best}}$ | $\sigma$ | Behaviour |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| random start | $\approx 5$ | $\approx 1.2$ | large steps, explores the whole square |
| stuck in a ripple | $\approx 2.6$ | $\approx 0.65$ | **still large enough to jump out** |
| inside the central dip | $\approx 10^{-3}$ | $\approx 10^{-4}$ | fine tuning |
| nearly solved | $\approx 10^{-9}$ | $\approx 10^{-10}$ | polishing the last decimals |

The rule is **self-correcting**: a run that is not improving keeps its steps large and keeps trying to escape,
while a run that is doing well automatically slows down and polishes. Section 25 measures the difference —
the trapped run disappears and the final accuracy improves by about three orders of magnitude.

## 9. The GA Sequence, Diversity, and Stopping

### 9.1 The ten steps

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Initialise** 50 random chromosomes $[x_1,x_2]$ inside $[-5,5]^{2}$ |
| 2 | **Evaluate** the Ackley value of every chromosome |
| 3 | **Sort** from best (smallest $f$) to worst |
| 4 | **Preserve the elite** — the single best chromosome |
| 5 | **Reproduce** — selection, crossover, mutation |
| 6 | **Roulette probabilities** — compute $p_i$ from fitness (drawn as a pie chart) |
| 7 | **Repair** — clip every new gene back into $[-5,5]$ |
| 8 | **Re-evaluate** — the Ackley value of each new offspring |
| 9 | **Form the next generation** — keep the best 50 candidates, elite guaranteed |
| 10 | **Repeat** until a stopping rule fires |

At step 9 the available candidates are the **50 parents plus the 50 offspring = 100 chromosomes**. We sort all
100 and keep the best 50. This holds the population size constant, guarantees a generation is never worse than
the last, and automatically preserves the elite.

### 9.2 One extra rule: cap duplicate copies

Truncating 100 candidates down to 50 is very strong pressure. With only 5% mutation most children are exact
copies of their parents, so in our first version the duplicates took over: by generation 11 all 50 chromosomes
were **literally the same point**. The GA had quietly stopped being a population method and become
elitism-plus-mutation on a single point — and a population of clones cannot explore its way out of anything.

The fix is one line in the survivor step:

> **No single chromosome may occupy more than 15 of the 50 seats.**

When a candidate would exceed its quota it is skipped and the seat goes to the next distinct candidate. If the
pool runs out of distinct candidates, the remaining seats are filled with fresh random chromosomes. This keeps
roughly 13 distinct chromosomes alive throughout the run instead of 2, at no cost in accuracy — §25 shows the
measurement.

### 9.3 Flow of one generation

```
        +-------------------------------+
        |  Initialise 50 chromosomes    |
        +---------------+---------------+
                        v
        +-------------------------------+
        |  Evaluate  f(x1,x2)   (50x)   |  <--------------------+
        +---------------+---------------+                       |
                        v                                       |
        +-------------------------------+                       |
        |  Sort  +  remember the elite  |                       |
        +---------------+---------------+                       |
                        v                                       |
                 stop?  --- yes --->  report the best solution  |
                        | no                                    |
                        v                                       |
        +------------------------------------+                  |
        |  sigma = best value / 4            |                  |
        |  fitness  ->  probabilities        |                  |
        |  roulette wheel   (50 spins)       |                  |
        |  1-point crossover  (Pc = 0.8)     |                  |
        |  mutation per gene  (Pm = 0.05)    |                  |
        |  clip into [-5, 5]                 |                  |
        +---------------+--------------------+                  |
                        v                                       |
        +-------------------------------------------------+     |
        | 100 candidates -> best 50, elite kept,          |-----+
        | no chromosome above 15 copies                   |
        +-------------------------------------------------+
```

### 9.4 Stopping criteria

Stop when **either** condition is met:

1. **100 generations** are complete, or
2. the improvement between consecutive generations stays below **$\text{tol}=10^{-10}$**.

The assignment gives no numeric tolerance, so we chose one. A single flat generation is normal in a GA —
mutation simply found nothing that round — so stopping at the first one would end the run far too early. We
require the improvement to stay below the tolerance for **20 consecutive generations** before declaring
convergence.

### 9.5 Settings used

| Parameter | Value | Source |
|---|---:|---|
| Population size | 50 | assignment |
| Maximum generations | 100 | assignment |
| Crossover probability $P_c$ | 0.80 | assignment |
| Mutation probability $P_m$ | 0.05 per gene | assignment |
| Elitism | 1 | assignment |
| Encoding | real value, 2 genes | assignment |
| Selection | roulette wheel | assignment |
| Crossover | 1 point | assignment |
| Mutation | Gaussian, then clipped | assignment |
| Search range | $[-5,5]$ | assignment |
| Survivor selection | best 50 of parents + offspring | assignment, step 9 |
| Mutation step $\sigma$ | $f_{\text{best}}/4$ | our choice, §8 |
| Maximum copies of one chromosome | 15 of 50 | our choice, §9.2 |
| Convergence tolerance | $10^{-10}$ held for 20 generations | our choice, §9.4 |
| Random seed | 7 | our choice, for reproducibility |

## 11. Step 2 — The Objective Function

`ackley` accepts either a single chromosome of shape `(2,)` or a whole population of shape `(50, 2)`: the
summations always run along the last axis, which is the gene axis.

=== CELL 14 code ===
def ackley(x, a=20.0, b=0.2, c=2.0 * math.pi):
    """Ackley objective value. Smaller is better.

    Parameters
    ----------
    x : array of shape (n_genes,) or (n_individuals, n_genes)
    """
    x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)
    n = x.shape[-1]

    mean_of_squares = np.sum(x ** 2, axis=-1) / n
    mean_of_cosines = np.sum(np.cos(c * x), axis=-1) / n

    exponential_part = -a * np.exp(-b * np.sqrt(mean_of_squares))
    cosine_part = -np.exp(mean_of_cosines)

    return exponential_part + cosine_part + a + math.e


GLOBAL_OPTIMUM = np.array([0.0, 0.0])

print("f(0, 0)           =", ackley(GLOBAL_OPTIMUM))
print("f(-4.0,  2.5)     =", ackley([-4.0, 2.5]))
print("f(-1.5, -1.0)     =", ackley([-1.5, -1.0]))

## 13. Steps 3 and 6 — Fitness and Roulette-Wheel Probabilities

Two small functions implement §6: `to_fitness` flips the direction of the objective value, and
`selection_probabilities` normalises so the 50 numbers sum to 1. The `total <= 0` guard covers the corner case
where the whole population has collapsed onto one value; then everyone gets an equal slice.

=== CELL 22 code ===
def to_fitness(values, kind="windowing"):
    """Turn objective values (lower is better) into fitness (higher is better)."""
    values = np.asarray(values, dtype=float)

    if kind == "inverse":
        return 1.0 / (1.0 + values)

    if kind == "windowing":
        worst, best = values.max(), values.min()
        offset = 0.10 * (worst - best) + 1e-12   # keeps the worst slice non-zero
        return (worst - values) + offset

    raise ValueError(f"unknown fitness kind: {kind!r}")


def selection_probabilities(values, kind="windowing"):
    """Step 6 - the slice of the roulette wheel owned by each chromosome."""
    fitness = to_fitness(values, kind)
    total = fitness.sum()
    if total <= 0:
        return np.full(len(fitness), 1.0 / len(fitness))
    return fitness / total

`roulette_wheel` below builds the cumulative rim once and "spins" it `k` times with `np.searchsorted`. Setting

=== CELL 24 code ===
def roulette_wheel(probabilities, rng, k):
    """Spin a fitness-proportional wheel k times; return the k chosen indices."""
    wheel = np.cumsum(probabilities)
    wheel[-1] = 1.0                      # guard against floating-point drift
    spins = rng.random(k)
    return np.searchsorted(wheel, spins, side="right")

## 14. Step 5a — One-Point Crossover

With two genes the cut `rng.integers(1, n_genes)` can only return `1`, the cut between $x_1$ and $x_2$. The
code is still written for a general chromosome length, so it would work unchanged on a 5-D or 10-D problem.

=== CELL 29 code ===
def one_point_crossover(parent_1, parent_2, rng, settings):
    """Step 5a - with probability pc, swap the tails of two parents."""
    if rng.random() > settings.pc:
        return parent_1.copy(), parent_2.copy()          # the 20% no-crossover case

    cut = int(rng.integers(1, len(parent_1)))             # always 1 when n_genes == 2
    child_1 = np.concatenate([parent_1[:cut], parent_2[cut:]])
    child_2 = np.concatenate([parent_2[:cut], parent_1[cut:]])
    return child_1, child_2

## 15. Steps 5b and 7 — Mutation, Step Size, and Boundary Repair

`mutation_sigma` implements §8. The default `"adaptive"` mode divides the current best objective value by 4;
the other two modes exist only so §27 can measure how much the adaptive rule actually buys.

The loop `for gene in range(len(mutated))` is the **array traversal** the assignment describes: each gene is
visited, gets its own random draw, and is changed only if that draw falls below $P_m$. The gene is clipped
immediately afterwards, which is Step 7.

=== CELL 31 code ===
def mutation_sigma(best_value, generation, settings):
    """Step size for Gaussian mutation. See section 8."""
    if settings.sigma_kind == "fixed":
        return settings.sigma_start

    if settings.sigma_kind == "schedule":            # the old clock-based version
        progress = generation / max(settings.max_generations, 1)
        return settings.sigma_start * (settings.sigma_end / settings.sigma_start) ** progress

    # "adaptive" - tie the step to how far we still are from the optimum
    sigma = settings.sigma_scale * best_value
    return min(max(sigma, settings.sigma_min), settings.sigma_max)


def mutate(chromosome, rng, settings, sigma):
    """Steps 5b and 7 - walk the genes, mutate with probability pm, then repair."""
    mutated = chromosome.copy()

    for gene in range(len(mutated)):                     # array traversal
        if rng.random() < settings.pm:
            if settings.mutation_kind == "uniform":
                mutated[gene] = rng.uniform(settings.lower, settings.upper)
            else:                                        # gaussian
                mutated[gene] += rng.normal(0.0, sigma)

            # Step 7 - the gene must stay inside the search space.
            mutated[gene] = min(max(mutated[gene], settings.lower), settings.upper)

    return mutated

## 16. Steps 5 to 8 — Producing One Batch of Offspring

`make_offspring` chains the three reproduction operators: turn objective values into selection probabilities,
spin the wheel to fill a mating pool, then walk the pool two parents at a time applying crossover, mutation and
clipping. It produces 50 children, which compete against their 50 parents in the next section.

=== CELL 33 code ===
def make_offspring(population, values, rng, settings, sigma):
    """Steps 5 to 7 - selection, crossover, mutation, repair."""
    probabilities = selection_probabilities(values, settings.fitness_kind)

    n_children = settings.population_size
    n_parents = n_children if n_children % 2 == 0 else n_children + 1
    mating_pool = population[roulette_wheel(probabilities, rng, n_parents)]

    children = []
    for i in range(0, n_parents, 2):
        child_1, child_2 = one_point_crossover(mating_pool[i], mating_pool[i + 1], rng, settings)
        children.append(mutate(child_1, rng, settings, sigma))
        children.append(mutate(child_2, rng, settings, sigma))

    return np.asarray(children[:n_children])

## 17. Steps 4 and 9 — Elitism, Survivors, and the Duplicate Cap

This implements assignment Step 9 — *keep the best 50 chromosomes from the available candidates while making
sure the elite is preserved* — plus the duplicate cap from §9.2.

1. pull the best `n_elites` parents out — the **elite**, in reserved seats;
2. put everyone else (49 parents and 50 children) into one pool of 99 candidates;
3. walk the pool from best to worst, taking each candidate **unless it already holds `max_copies` seats**;
4. if the pool runs out of eligible candidates, fill the rest with fresh random chromosomes;
5. sort the final 50 so index 0 is always the best.

Because the elite never competes for its seat, the best solution found so far can never be lost.

`survivor_kind` also offers `"offspring_only"`, where parents are discarded each round. It is measured in §27.

=== CELL 35 code ===
def select_survivors(population, values, offspring, offspring_values, rng, settings):
    """Steps 4 and 9 - elites plus the best of the candidate pool, duplicates capped."""
    ranking = np.argsort(values, kind="stable")
    elite_index = ranking[:settings.n_elites]
    elites, elite_values = population[elite_index], values[elite_index]   # Step 4

    if settings.survivor_kind == "offspring_only":
        pool, pool_values = offspring, offspring_values
    else:                                                 # parents_plus_offspring
        pool = np.vstack([np.delete(population, elite_index, axis=0), offspring])
        pool_values = np.concatenate([np.delete(values, elite_index), offspring_values])

    order = np.argsort(pool_values, kind="stable")        # best first
    pool, pool_values = pool[order], pool_values[order]
    free_seats = settings.population_size - settings.n_elites

    if settings.max_copies > 0:
        # Count how many seats each distinct chromosome already holds. Using the raw
        # bytes of the row as a dictionary key gives an exact-duplicate test.
        seats_used = {}
        for elite in elites:
            key = elite.tobytes()
            seats_used[key] = seats_used.get(key, 0) + 1

        chosen_rows = []
        for i in range(len(pool)):
            key = pool[i].tobytes()
            if seats_used.get(key, 0) < settings.max_copies:
                seats_used[key] = seats_used.get(key, 0) + 1
                chosen_rows.append(i)
                if len(chosen_rows) == free_seats:
                    break
        chosen_rows = np.asarray(chosen_rows, dtype=int)
    else:
        chosen_rows = np.arange(min(free_seats, len(pool)))

    survivors, survivor_values = pool[chosen_rows], pool_values[chosen_rows]

    # If the cap left seats empty, fill them with fresh random chromosomes.
    missing = free_seats - len(survivors)
    if missing > 0:
        newcomers = rng.uniform(settings.lower, settings.upper,
                                size=(missing, settings.n_genes))
        survivors = np.vstack([survivors, newcomers]) if len(survivors) else newcomers
        survivor_values = np.concatenate([survivor_values, ackley(newcomers)])

    new_population = np.vstack([elites, survivors])
    new_values = np.concatenate([elite_values, survivor_values])

    final_order = np.argsort(new_values, kind="stable")   # Step 3 - keep it sorted
    return new_population[final_order], new_values[final_order]

## 18. Step 10 — The Main Loop

`run_ga` assembles the sequence and records a history, so every figure in Part 3 comes from one run.

Inside each generation: record the state, check the two stopping rules, build and evaluate 50 offspring, form
the next generation, then update the best-so-far solution and the stagnation counter. `stagnation` resets to
zero whenever a generation improves the best value by more than `tolerance`, and increments otherwise; when it
reaches `patience`, stopping rule 2 fires.

=== CELL 37 code ===
def run_ga(settings=SETTINGS, verbose=True, snapshot_at=()):
    """Run the complete genetic algorithm and return everything worth reporting."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(settings.seed)

    population = create_population(rng, settings)                 # Step 1
    values = ackley(population)                                   # Step 2
    order = np.argsort(values, kind="stable")                     # Step 3
    population, values = population[order], values[order]

    best_solution, best_value, best_generation = population[0].copy(), float(values[0]), 0

    history = {key: [] for key in
               ("generation", "best", "mean", "worst", "best_x1", "best_x2",
                "spread", "sigma", "distinct")}
    snapshots = {}
    stagnation = 0
    stop_reason = f"reached the maximum of {settings.max_generations} generations"

    for generation in range(settings.max_generations + 1):
        sigma = mutation_sigma(best_value, generation, settings)

        history["generation"].append(generation)
        history["best"].append(best_value)
        history["mean"].append(float(values.mean()))
        history["worst"].append(float(values.max()))
        history["best_x1"].append(float(best_solution[0]))
        history["best_x2"].append(float(best_solution[1]))
        history["spread"].append(float(np.mean(np.std(population, axis=0))))
        history["sigma"].append(sigma)
        history["distinct"].append(int(len(np.unique(population, axis=0))))
        if generation in snapshot_at:
            snapshots[generation] = population.copy()

        # --- stopping criteria ---------------------------------------
        if generation == settings.max_generations:
            break
        if stagnation >= settings.patience:
            stop_reason = (f"improvement stayed below {settings.tolerance:g} "
                           f"for {settings.patience} generations in a row")
            break

        offspring = make_offspring(population, values, rng, settings, sigma)   # Steps 5-7
        offspring_values = ackley(offspring)                                   # Step 8
        population, values = select_survivors(population, values, offspring,
                                              offspring_values, rng, settings)  # Steps 4, 9

        improvement = best_value - float(values[0])
        if improvement > 0.0:
            best_value = float(values[0])
            best_solution = population[0].copy()
            best_generation = generation + 1
        stagnation = stagnation + 1 if improvement < settings.tolerance else 0

    result = {
        "best_solution": best_solution,
        "best_value": best_value,
        "best_generation": best_generation,
        "generations_run": history["generation"][-1],
        "stop_reason": stop_reason,
        "history": {key: np.asarray(value) for key, value in history.items()},
        "population": population,
        "values": values,
        "snapshots": snapshots,
        "settings": settings,
    }

    if verbose:
        print("stopped because : " + stop_reason)
        print("generations run : %d" % result["generations_run"])
        print("best found at   : generation %d" % best_generation)
        print("best x1         : %+.14f" % best_solution[0])
        print("best x2         : %+.14f" % best_solution[1])
        print("best f(x1, x2)  : %.12e" % best_value)

    return result

### What the two plots say

**Left — convergence.** A steep drop over the first ~20 generations, where selection and crossover spread the
few good coordinates that already existed in the random start. Then a staircase: flat stretches where mutation
found nothing, and sudden drops where it did. Flat stretches are normal, and they are why the stopping rule
waits for 20 of them in a row rather than one. The blue curve never rises — that is elitism.

**Right — how the search narrows.** The green dashed line is the mutation step, now driven by the best value
rather than by the clock, and the purple line is how widely the population is actually spread. They track each
other closely, which is exactly the intended behaviour: the step size is always about the size of the gap that
is left.

The red line is the number of **distinct** chromosomes. It settles around a dozen and never collapses to 1, so
the population stays a genuine population for the whole run. §25 shows what this looked like before the
duplicate cap was added.

**On the "20-25 generations" note in the assignment sheet.** The table above makes it concrete. By generation
20 the best value is already about $6	imes10^{-3}$, with $x_1$ and $x_2$ both within a few thousandths of
zero. On any plot of the search space the answer is *visually* found by then, and that is the practical
convergence region the sheet refers to.

Everything after roughly generation 25 is no longer searching; it is adding decimal places to an answer we
already have. We still run the full 100 generations because the assignment asks us to report **precision**, and
those last 75 generations are exactly what turn two or three correct decimals into twelve.

### Reading the table

**Windowing is scale-invariant.** Its ratio between the biggest and smallest slice stays pinned at 11.00 even
though the objective values have fallen by twelve orders of magnitude. That is the whole point of measuring
fitness as a *window*, $f_{\max}-f_i$: what matters is how a chromosome compares with its current rivals, not
how large $f$ happens to be. (In the last row the ratio dips below 11 only because the values have shrunk to
the size of the safety term $\varepsilon=10^{-12}$ inside $\delta$, which then stops being negligible.)

**The inverse transform $1/(1+f)$ is not.** Once every $f$ is small, every fitness is close to 1, the ratio
collapses to 1.00, and the wheel becomes uniform — selection stops distinguishing good from mediocre. That is
a real weakness on a minimisation problem whose optimum is zero, and it is why windowing is our default.

## 25. Two Weaknesses We Found, and How We Fixed Them

Our first working version used the two obvious choices: a **clock-based mutation schedule** ($\sigma$ shrinking
from 1 to $10^{-8}$ by generation 100) and **plain truncation** of the 100 candidates down to 50. It reached
$f \approx 10^{-9}$ and looked fine. Running it 40 times exposed two problems.

**Weakness 1 — the population became clones.** By generation 11 all 50 chromosomes were the same point. The GA
had stopped being a population method.

**Weakness 2 — one run in 40 was trapped.** It finished at $f \approx 2.58$, sitting on the first ring of local
minima, because $\sigma$ had shrunk below the distance needed to escape long before the run ended.

The two fixes are described in §8 (adaptive $\sigma$) and §9.2 (duplicate cap). The cell below switches each
one on and off independently — a 2 × 2 comparison on the same 40 seeds — so we can see what each one is
actually responsible for.

### What the comparison shows

Reading the table column by column separates the two effects cleanly.

* **The duplicate cap owns the diversity column.** Under either $\sigma$ rule it lifts the number of distinct
  chromosomes several times over. It does *not* fix the trapped run: with the clock schedule, the worst result

* **The adaptive step size owns the other three columns.** It removes the trapped run — every run now ends in

  proportionally-sized step keeps producing genuinely different children.

The two rows with adaptive $\sigma$ land within a small factor of each other, so the cap is close to free. We
keep it because diversity is *insurance*: a population of clones has no way to recover if it is ever in the
wrong place, and the cost of carrying that insurance is almost nothing.

Both fixes are small — one changes how $\sigma$ is computed, the other adds a quota when choosing survivors —
and neither touches any parameter the assignment specifies.

=== CELL 64 markdown ===
### What the comparison shows

* **The mutation step rule is the single biggest factor.** A fixed step stalls at roughly its own size, and the
  clock-based schedule lands three orders of magnitude short of the adaptive rule.
* **Uniform mutation** replaces a gene with a fresh random value — great for escaping ripples, hopeless for
  fine tuning, because a child sitting $10^{-12}$ from the optimum gets thrown back into $[-5,5]$.
* **The duplicate cap** is roughly accuracy-neutral, as §25 already showed. Its value is diversity, not
  precision.
* **The inverse fitness transform** still converges, but ends short of windowing, for the reason in §24: its
  wheel goes uniform once all the values are small.
* **Offspring-only survivors** discard good parents every round. That keeps diversity high but loses several
  orders of magnitude of precision — the exploration/exploitation trade-off in one line.
* **Without crossover** the GA still converges, but noticeably less far, because nothing recombines a good
  $x_1$ found by one chromosome with a good $x_2$ found by another.
* **Without mutation** it fails completely. With two genes, crossover can only re-pair coordinates that already
  exist, so with no new numbers the search is confined forever to the $50\times50$ grid of coordinates present
  at generation 0.
* **Without elitism** (and with parents discarded, so the best really can be lost) the result becomes unstable.

Every operator in the specification has a distinct job, and removing any one damages a different part of the
behaviour.

## 29. Conclusion

**The task.** Minimise the 2-D Ackley function over $[-5,5]^2$ with a genetic algorithm using value encoding,
roulette-wheel selection, one-point crossover, mutation and elitism of 1, with a population of 50 and at most
100 generations.

**The outcome.** The GA located the global minimum with an objective error of about $10^{-12}$, using roughly
5000 function evaluations, and all 40 independent runs reached it.

| Component | Its job in this problem |
|---|---|
| Value encoding | a gene holds any real number, so precision is not capped by a bit-width |
| Fitness transform | turns "small $f$ is good" into "large fitness is good" for the wheel |
| Roulette-wheel selection | better chromosomes get more children, weak ones keep a small chance |
| One-point crossover | pairs a good $x_1$ from one parent with a good $x_2$ from another |
| Gaussian mutation | creates values the population never held — the only way out of a ripple |
| Adaptive $\sigma=f_{\text{best}}/4$ | steps always match the distance still to cover |
| Clipping | keeps every gene legal, inside $[-5,+5]$ |
| Elitism = 1 | the best solution can never be lost |
| Best 50 of 100, max 15 copies | a generation is never worse than the last, and never becomes clones |

**What we learned about the method**

* A GA needs no gradient and no assumption about the shape of the function — only the ability to compare two
  candidates. That is why it transfers to problems where no formula exists at all.
* Its accuracy is limited by the size of the random step it can still take, which is why the mutation rule,
  not the population size, was the decisive parameter here.
* A first version that *works* is not the same as one that works *reliably*. The two weaknesses in §25 were
  only visible after running the algorithm 40 times, and both fixes were a few lines each.
